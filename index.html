Work I do on my own time

Three lines, all ongoing and unpublished. The first has a full test report; the other two are still being written up. These are the problems I want to keep working on — in a doctorate, or in a team that takes them seriously.

Machine learning for non-technical loss

Can a distribution network be made to balance its own books, so that unexplained energy points at specific meters?

Non-technical loss — theft, meter tampering, billing faults — is the part of distribution loss that inspection alone cannot locate, and untargeted inspection loses money, which is why utilities tolerate it. Earlier work on this shipped to production and cut confirmed theft losses by 3%.

The current model, PI-STGAT, puts the feeder energy balance inside the loss function as a physical constraint rather than treating the transformer reading as one more input feature. It therefore has to attribute the unexplained residual to individual meters, and reports estimated stolen kilowatt-hours instead of a bare probability. Where it fails is also measured: theft that shifts consumption without reducing the total violates no conservation law and stays largely invisible.

fig. 3 — about

How I got here

I came to data from hardware. My BSc is in electrical engineering, specialising in electronics and automation, and I spent my first years after graduating as an electrical engineer in the energy sector — where the work turned steadily into data work. I built SARIMAX and ARIMA demand forecasts that tightened equipment inventory planning, and the first production version of the theft-detection system that became my longest-running research project.

That shift sent me back to university for an MSc in mechatronic engineering, specialising in AI and robotics, with a thesis on multi-agent decision making under reinforcement learning.

Since 2023 I have worked as a data scientist across confectionery manufacturing and ERP systems, concentrating on natural language processing, large language models, time series, and finance.

Teaching is the other half of the job. Mentoring junior data scientists and students is where the guides above come from: each one started as an answer I kept repeating, written down properly once.
